When you deposit $100 in a bank, the bank does not simply hold it. Under fractional reserve banking, it is required to keep only a fraction — historically 10% in the United States — and is permitted to lend out the rest. That $90 loan gets spent, deposited in another bank, and the process repeats. Each bank lends out 90% of what it receives. The original $100 generates a chain of deposits and loans that, in theory, can expand the total money circulating in the economy to $1,000.

This is not a trick or a conspiracy. It is the standard model taught in every introductory economics course, endorsed by central banks, and practiced in some form in nearly every country with a modern banking system. The mainstream case for it is that it allows a small base of savings to fund a much larger amount of productive activity — mortgages, business loans, infrastructure investment.

The questions worth asking are different ones: Who decides the reserve rate? (Regulators — and in the US, that rate is currently 0%.) What kinds of things can get a loan? (Things with collateral and a projected cash flow — not a wetland, not a decade of unpaid care work, not a forest.) What happens to the interest? (It must be repaid — but only the principal was created. The interest has to come from somewhere, and that somewhere is always new debt.)

Understanding the model

Mainstream case for it

The expansion of credit is treated in standard economics as a feature that enables economic activity to scale beyond what any fixed stock of money could support. The reserve requirement is the safety valve — the lever regulators use to prevent the multiplier from running out of control.

  • Allows a small base of deposits to support a much larger economy
  • Banks act as efficient intermediaries between savers and borrowers
  • Credit creation funds investment, business formation, and home ownership
  • Reserve requirement is a regulatory lever — the multiplier is controllable
  • Standard in every functioning modern economy
This is the textbook framing. The mechanism is presented as a feature, not a flaw.
Structural critiques

These are not fringe arguments. Post-2008, they appear in mainstream policy discussions and in explicit statements from central banks. The Bank of England published a paper in 2014 stating plainly that banks create money through lending — not the other way around.

  • Money creation is delegated to private institutions, not democratic bodies
  • New money only enters the economy as debt — it is borrowed into existence
  • Credit flows toward collateralizable assets (real estate, equipment), not toward ecological or care infrastructure with no transaction price
  • Systemic fragility: the cascade can run in reverse (bank runs, credit freezes)
  • Reserve requirement can be — and has been — set to zero
These critiques appear in heterodox economics (Keen, Hudson, MMT) and in post-2008 mainstream re-evaluation.
Mathematical concern

The multiplier is bounded — the series converges, not to infinity but to 1 ÷ reserve rate. The more pointed mathematical concern is about interest. When money is created as debt, only the principal enters circulation. The interest charged on that principal was never created. At the system level, this produces a structural growth requirement.

  • Principal is created when a loan is made. Interest is not.
  • If $1,000 is created at 5% interest, $1,050 must be repaid — but only $1,000 exists
  • The $50 gap must come from someone else taking on new debt
  • At the system level, total debt must always grow to service existing debt
  • In a physically finite world, this is a structural tension, not just a policy choice
Note: banks spend interest income back into the economy (salaries, dividends), which partially recirculates it. The concern is systemic, not about any individual loan.

Reserve requirements in major economies — current as of 2024

Market Requirement Notes
United States 0% Eliminated March 2020 by the Federal Reserve. Not reinstated. Capital requirements (Basel III) now do the regulatory work.
United Kingdom 0% Abolished 1981. Replaced entirely by capital adequacy rules. The Bank of England explicitly states banks create money through lending.
Canada 0% Abolished 1992. No formal reserve requirement.
Australia 0% No formal requirement. Liquidity coverage ratio applies instead.
Eurozone (ECB) 1% Reduced from 2% in 2012. Applies to overnight deposits and deposits with agreed maturity up to 2 years.
Japan 0.8–1.3% Varies by deposit type and size of institution.
India 4.5% Called the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR). Used actively as a monetary policy tool.
China ~6% The People's Bank of China adjusts this frequently. Was 13%+ before 2019 rate cuts. Still an active policy lever.
Brazil ~20% One of the highest in major economies. Demand deposits carry higher requirements than time deposits.

The 10% figure used in this model reflects historical US practice and remains a common teaching standard. Most high-income economies have replaced reserve requirements with capital requirements (Basel III/IV), which regulate the ratio of a bank's capital to its risk-weighted assets — a related but distinct mechanism.
