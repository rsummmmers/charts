The Offset Problem

Carbon offsets and cap-and-trade start from the assumption that damage is normal. That's a different problem than they solve.

Offset systems and cap-and-trade programs are often described as market-based solutions to ecological harm. They are mechanisms for managing harm at a price. Step through the logic of each — what they do, what they don't do, and what assumption they share that a fuller accounting would replace.

Carbon offsets
You emit here. Someone plants trees there. The accounting says net zero. The atmosphere receives the emission now. The trees (if they survive, aren't cut, don't burn) absorb carbon over decades. The offset is a financial instrument, not a physical one — and it starts from the premise that the emission was going to happen.
Cap and trade
A government sets a total emissions cap and issues permits up to that limit. Companies that emit less can sell permits to companies that emit more. Total emissions stay at the cap. This is a real constraint — but the cap is set politically, often higher than ecological limits, and it permits harm up to the cap by design. It optimizes the distribution of a fixed amount of damage.
A fuller accounting
Instead of starting from "damage is happening, how do we offset or distribute it?" — asks what the living system is worth intact. What does a forest do that cannot be replaced by a financial instrument? What is the full cost of losing it, including the cycling functions, the threshold risk, the irreversibility? This changes the baseline from damage-assumed to value-intact.
What offsets and cap-and-trade share
Both accept that ecological harm will occur and create mechanisms to manage it. Neither asks whether the harm should happen. Neither accounts for threshold failure — a coral reef near collapse isn't made safer by purchasing an offset. Neither captures irreversibility — a species lost isn't recovered by someone planting a tree. Both start from inside the standard ledger.
The baseline problem
An offset says: "compared to what would have happened without intervention, this action reduces harm by X." The baseline — what would have happened — is often fictional, unverifiable, and locked in at the moment of the transaction. Forests counted as offsets have burned. Projects counted as protecting land have been deforested. The instrument works on paper because the baseline is on paper.
