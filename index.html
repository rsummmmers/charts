Visualizations of Money, Debt, and Growth Implications

Eight interactive models: money creation, the interest mechanism, the physical substrate, regulatory limits, and systems dynamics

These models can be used in sequence or independently. Start with #1 for the mechanics of money creation; #3 and #4 for the structural argument about interest; #5 for Herman Daly's critique of what the standard model leaves out; #6 for the distinction between reserve and capital requirements; #7 for why historical risk modeling fails in a non-stationary world; #8 for stock vs. flow dynamics using water.

The Money Multiplier
How $100 becomes $1,000 through the cascade of deposits and loans. The math is bounded — not infinite — but every dollar in the chain is someone's debt. Interactive slider shows how reserve requirements control the multiplier.

Daly's Missing Substrate
The standard economic circular flow shows households and firms exchanging money for goods and services — but shows nothing about where the goods come from. Step through what the model omits: the biosphere, resource throughput, solar energy, and entropy.

6
Reserve vs. Capital Requirements
Two different guardrails protecting against two different bank failures — and what neither one addresses. Reserve requirements (currently 0% in the US) protect against bank runs. Capital requirements protect against loan defaults. Both are silent on the structural interest gap.
Liquidity vs. solvency · Basel III · What the guardrails don't cover
→
7
Risk Models and a Changing World
Financial risk models assume the future will look statistically like the past. In a non-stationary world — where climate shifts baselines, ecological tipping points change frequencies, and "100-year events" happen every decade — this assumption fails silently. The model outputs a number that looks correct. It isn't.
Stationarity assumption · VaR · Distribution shift · The silent failure mode
→
8
Stock vs Flow Systems
Three system types — flow (a river), stock (a reservoir), and hybrid (topsoil, a fishery, a working forest) — with fundamentally different dynamics and failure modes. Flow fails instantly and visibly. Stock fails slowly, then suddenly. Hybrid can tip at a threshold and never recover. Examples across water, ecology, industry, and economy.
Systems thinking · Hybrid systems · Threshold failure · Ecological assets
→
9
The Incomplete Ledger
Every accounting system draws a boundary. What's inside gets counted. What's outside doesn't exist — not because it isn't real, but because the ledger wasn't designed to see it. Step through what the standard economic ledger leaves out: ecological services, care work, future generations. The ledger always looks complete from inside it.
Ledger boundary · Externalities · Care work · What gets counted
→
10
Threshold Failure
Two systems under the same pressure. One degrades proportionally — continuous feedback, reversible. The other holds a stable state until a threshold is crossed, then collapses to a new lower state that doesn't reverse when pressure is removed. Most living systems are the second kind. Most management frameworks assume the first. Interactive pressure slider.
Regime shift · Non-linear collapse · Hysteresis · Living systems
