Visualizations of Money, Debt, and Growth Implications

Twenty-seven interactive models in five groups

Each model stands alone — you can start anywhere. The first group defines the vocabulary. Then: how banks create money and why interest creates a built-in growth problem; what rules exist and what they can't fix; what the economy leaves out of its accounting; and how biophysical systems actually work.

Money Concept Definitions Eight foundational words — and how they relate to each other
1
Value
Usefulness. A tomato you grew has value because it feeds someone. Value is not the same as price — price is what someone pays at a specific moment; value is what a thing actually does. Value exists even when no money changes hands.
Usefulness · value ≠ price · no money required
→
2
Wealth
Stored-up capacity to meet needs — tangible and intangible. A home you own. Good health. Skills that regenerate income. Relationships with people who would show up for you. All real wealth, even though none appear in a bank account.
Tangible · human · social · security · wealth ≠ money
→
3
Money
A coordination tool. It lets millions of strangers trade without swapping goods directly. What makes it unusual: it only works because everyone agrees it does. A pile of cash in a town where no one accepts it is worthless. The agreement is the money.
Coordination tool · shared agreement · stranger trade · money ≠ wealth
→
4
Capital
Wealth or resources that make MORE value. A pump, a factory, a truck. So is a seed, a skilled worker, healthy soil. The key distinction: capital produces. Wealth just holds. An apple is wealth. The apple tree is capital — it keeps producing.
Produces value · capital vs. wealth · farm · industry
→
5
Asset
Anything you own that holds value or earns income. Land, tools, savings, a house. Not all assets are capital: an orchard is both an asset and capital. Savings are an asset but not capital — they hold value but don't make more.
Holds value · earns income · asset vs. capital · overlap indicators
→
6
Debt
Borrowed value that must be paid back — usually with interest. You receive something now and owe back more than you received. The interest gap is real: more is always owed back than was created, which is why debt-based economies require continuous growth.
Borrowed value · interest gap · more owed than exists · growth requirement
→
7
Credit
Your permission to borrow — your reputation for paying back. Good credit means you can borrow more at lower cost. No credit means you cannot borrow at all. Credit is not money. It is the door that lets you access debt.
Permission to borrow · reputation · credit ≠ money · the debt door
→
8
Price
Not the same as value. Price is what someone agrees to pay at one specific moment — shaped by supply, urgency, and bargaining power. The same tomato is $2.99 at a grocery store, $0 from a neighbor's garden, and $8.00 after a drought. The value (it feeds you) doesn't change. The price swings with context.
Price ≠ value · same thing, different context · moment of exchange
→
9
How They Connect
These eight terms form a system. Value gets stored as wealth. Wealth gets represented as money. Capital uses wealth to produce more value. Assets hold value or produce income. Debt is borrowed money. Credit opens the debt door. Price measures value at a specific moment. When someone says "I have money," they might mean any of these.
Value · wealth · money · capital · asset · debt · credit · price · the relationships
→
How Banks Create Money The mechanics of money creation, and why interest creates a built-in problem
1
The Money Multiplier
Your $100 deposit becomes $1,000 in loans — how? Banks lend out most of what you deposit. The next person deposits that money somewhere else. That bank lends it out again. Every step creates new money, but every dollar created starts as someone's debt. Move the slider to see how the rules change the math.
How loans create new money · every dollar is a debt · the reserve rule
→
2
The Debt Copy Problem
Banks create the amount you borrowed — but not the extra you owe as interest. So where does the interest come from? This model uses a simple game to show why the system always has more owed than it has money to pay. This isn't a mistake or a scam. It's how the system was designed.
Why there's always more owed than exists · where the interest gap comes from
→
3
The Interest Ratchet
When you pay back a loan with interest, you pay back more than was created. But no one created the extra. This model shows how the gap between money that exists and money that is owed keeps growing — and can only grow. Drag the rate and time sliders to see how fast.
Growing gap · more owed than exists · drag to explore
→
4
The Interest Variable
Every detail stays the same except one: whether loans carry interest. Toggle back and forth to see exactly where the problem enters the picture. Everything else is identical. Only interest changes.
One variable changed · A/B comparison · isolates the mechanism

How the Financial System Is Governed What controls exist, what they can and can't fix, and what a different design looks like
6
Three Banking Guardrails
Banks operate under three sets of rules designed to prevent three types of failure: bank runs (everyone tries to withdraw at once), bad loans (too many go unpaid), and insolvency (the bank owes more than it has). Step through each guardrail — who sets it, what it covers — and what all three have in common: none of them address the interest gap.
Bank run · loan default · insolvency · what the rules cover · what they don't
→
7
Risk Models and a Changing World
Banks use math models to predict how often bad things will happen. These models look at the past 20–30 years and assume the future will look the same. In a stable world, this works. But when the climate is shifting, so-called "once in a hundred years" events can start happening every ten years. The model prints a number that looks right. It isn't.
Past data · shifting baselines · when the model is wrong · climate and finance
→
12
Public vs. Private Credit Creation
Both public banks and private banks create money the same way. The difference is where the interest goes. Private banks: interest goes to shareholders. Public banks: interest goes back to the public. That one change affects whether the system requires continuous private growth just to stay afloat. Step through the mechanism and trace where the money goes.
Where interest goes · public bank · the growth requirement · Bank of North Dakota
→
13
The Ponzi Comparison
A Ponzi scheme (named after Charles Ponzi, who ran a famous fraud in 1920) needs a constant stream of new money to pay old promises. An interest-bearing banking system needs continuous new growth to service existing loans. These two systems share a structural feature. They differ in intent, in what they produce, and in what happens when growth stops. Step through both to see where they match — and where they don't.
Same structure · different intent · requires growth · what happens when it stops

What the Economy Leaves Out The hidden foundation the economy runs on — and doesn't pay for
5
Daly's Missing Substrate
The standard picture of the economy shows money moving between households and businesses — but nothing about where goods come from. Herman Daly (1938–2022), an ecological economist, pointed out what's missing: the living world, the sun's energy, resources that get used up, and waste that has to go somewhere. Step through what the model doesn't show.
The standard economic model · what's left out · Herman Daly · the living world
→
9
The Incomplete Ledger
Every accounting system draws a line: what's inside gets counted, what's outside doesn't exist — not because it isn't real, but because the accounting wasn't built to see it. Step through what the standard economic ledger leaves out: the work of nature, the work of caregivers, and the costs passed on to people in the future.
What gets counted · costs left off the bill · care work · future generations
→
14
The Hidden Foundation
The economy runs on three inputs it doesn't fully pay for: 300 million years of stored solar energy in fossil fuels (priced only at the cost of getting it out, not replacing it); $10–39 trillion per year of care work done mostly by women (invisible to GDP); and $125–145 trillion per year of services provided by living ecosystems (invisible until they fail). The visible economy sits on a foundation it doesn't pay for.
Fossil fuel subsidy · unpaid care work · ecosystem services · hidden transfer · what's not in the price
→
15
Bucket-nomics
A rain-harvesting system costs more upfront than buying water from a pipe. Standard economic analysis says it doesn't "make sense" — because it compares the upfront cost to today's water price. That price doesn't include the ecological cost of where the water comes from, or how the price will change over the next 20 years. This model charts what happens when you include the full picture.
Upfront cost · backward-looking pricing · full accounting · 20-year chart

How Biophysical Systems Work The physical rules nature runs on — and why they don't respond to economic tools the way we assume
8
Stock vs. Flow Systems
Three types of water systems: a flowing river (only exists while it's raining), a rain barrel (stores water, drains when used), and a living pond (stores water AND helps maintain the conditions that let it hold more). Each responds differently when the rain stops. Most economic models assume everything works like the barrel. Most living systems are the pond.
Flow · stock · hybrid · when the rain stops · different failure modes
→
10
Threshold Failure
Two systems under the same pressure. One gets worse gradually — pull back the stress and it recovers. The other holds steady, then suddenly collapses to a much worse state — and doesn't recover when the stress is removed. Most living systems are the second kind. Most policies assume the first. Interactive pressure slider.
Gradual vs. sudden · collapse · doesn't reverse · how living systems break
→
11
The Offset Problem
Carbon offsets and cap-and-trade let companies pay to damage the environment. They are tools for managing harm at a price — not for questioning whether the harm should happen. Neither accounts for the fact that living systems can cross a point of no return. Step through what each does, what both miss, and what a different starting point looks like.
Carbon offsets · cap and trade · you can't buy back a tipping point · the baseline question
→
16
Entropy: Why Things Only Go One Way
Mix cream into coffee and you can't un-mix it. Tear a piece of bread and it doesn't repair itself. Burn wood and you can't turn the smoke back into wood. This is entropy — the physical law that says energy and matter always move toward disorder, never backward. Every economic process runs on energy, and every use of energy produces waste heat that can't be recaptured. Understanding entropy means understanding why "recycling" and "efficiency" have hard limits.
Entropy · energy quality · disorder · why extraction is a one-way door · thermodynamic limits
→
17
Gravity, Atmosphere, and the Water Cycle
Gravity holds our atmosphere in place — without it, the gases that make breathing possible would drift into space. It pulls water down through watersheds, drives the water cycle, and shapes every physical system on Earth. These are the background conditions every economy runs inside of — they didn't appear on the invoice because no one made them, and no one can replace them. They are not resources. They are the container.
Gravity · atmosphere · water cycle · background conditions · what we can't replace
→
18
Emergence
A murmuration of starlings moves as one fluid shape — but no single bird is in charge. A brain produces thought, but no individual neuron thinks. A forest regulates its own climate, but no single tree controls the weather. Emergence happens when a group of simple parts produces something that none of the parts could produce alone. Living systems are full of it. Economic models largely ignore it — which is one reason they fail to predict how ecosystems respond to stress.
Emergence · the whole is more than its parts · self-organization · what models miss
