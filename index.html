Visualizations of Money, Debt, and Growth Implications

Thirteen interactive models: money creation, the interest mechanism, the physical substrate, regulatory limits, and systems dynamics

These models can be used in sequence or independently. Start with #1 for the mechanics of money creation; #3 and #4 for the structural argument about interest; #5 for Herman Daly's critique of what the standard model leaves out; #6 for the three regulatory guardrails and their limits; #7 for why historical risk modeling fails in a non-stationary world; #8 for stock vs. flow dynamics; #12 for how the money-creation mechanism itself can be redesigned; #13 for how the structural growth imperative compares to a Ponzi scheme.

Three Banking Guardrails
Reserve requirements, capital requirements, and loan loss reserves (ACL) protect against three different failure modes: bank runs, loan defaults, and expected credit losses. Each is set by a different mechanism. All three are silent on the structural interest gap.
Liquidity · Solvency · Credit quality · OCC / Fed / FDIC · Basel III · What the guardrails don't cover

7
Risk Models and a Changing World
Financial risk models assume the future will look statistically like the past. In a non-stationary world — where climate shifts baselines, ecological tipping points change frequencies, and "100-year events" happen every decade — this assumption fails silently. The model outputs a number that looks correct. It isn't.
Stationarity assumption · VaR · Distribution shift · The silent failure mode
→
8
Stock vs Flow Systems
Three system types — flow (a river), stock (a reservoir), and hybrid (topsoil, a fishery, a working forest) — with fundamentally different dynamics and failure modes. Flow fails instantly and visibly. Stock fails slowly, then suddenly. Hybrid can tip at a threshold and never recover. It's not the bucket. It's the rain.
Systems thinking · Hybrid systems · Threshold failure · Ecological assets
→
9
The Incomplete Ledger
Every accounting system draws a boundary. What's inside gets counted. What's outside doesn't exist — not because it isn't real, but because the ledger wasn't designed to see it. Step through what the standard economic ledger leaves out: ecological services, care work, future generations. The ledger always looks complete from inside it.
Ledger boundary · Externalities · Care work · What gets counted
→
10
Threshold Failure
Two systems under the same pressure. One degrades proportionally — continuous feedback, reversible. The other holds a stable state until a threshold is crossed, then collapses to a new lower state that doesn't reverse when pressure is removed. Most living systems are the second kind. Most management frameworks assume the first. Interactive pressure slider.
Regime shift · Non-linear collapse · Hysteresis · Living systems
→
11
The Offset Problem
Carbon offsets and cap-and-trade are mechanisms for managing harm at a price — not for questioning whether the harm should happen. Both start from a baseline assumption that damage is normal. Neither accounts for threshold failure, irreversibility, or the full intact value of a living system. Step through what each does, what both miss, and what a different baseline looks like.
Carbon offsets · Cap and trade · Baseline problem · Fuller accounting
→
12
Public vs. Private Credit Creation
Both systems create money as debt. The difference is in who captures the interest — private shareholders or the public treasury. This single variable changes whether the system requires continuous private-sector growth to remain solvent. Step through the mechanism, trace where the interest goes, and see what changes when the money-creation design changes.
Sovereign issuance · Public banks · Interest destination · Growth imperative · Bank of North Dakota
→
13
The Ponzi Comparison
A Ponzi scheme and the interest-bearing banking system share a structural feature: both require continuous new growth to service existing obligations. They differ in intent, production, and what happens at ecological limits. Step through the Ponzi mechanics, then the banking mechanics, then the comparison — and see where the distinction narrows.
Structural similarity · Intent vs. consequence · Production vs. no production · Ecological limits · Collapse conditions
