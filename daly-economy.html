The Missing Substrate

What the standard economic model cannot see.

Every introductory economics course teaches the circular flow model: households supply labor, firms pay wages, households spend on goods, firms produce goods. Money and goods circulate in a loop. The model is elegant, internally consistent, and widely taught as a description of how economies work.

Herman Daly's critique is simple: the circular flow model treats the economy as a closed system. It has no inlet and no outlet. But real economies are not closed systems. They extract low-entropy matter and energy from the physical world, transform it into goods and services, and return high-entropy waste to the physical world. The model makes this throughput invisible. Step through the layers below to see what gets added back in.

Daly's critique — the key ideas

The economy is a subsystem, not the system
Standard economics treats the economy as the whole system and nature as one of many inputs (a "factor of production"). Daly inverts this: the economy is a subsystem of the biosphere, which is itself a subsystem of the thermodynamic system bounded by solar energy in and heat out. You cannot have infinite economic growth within a finite biosphere any more than you can have infinite growth of any subsystem within a finite system.
Throughput: the invisible variable
The circular flow model shows money and goods going around in loops. What it hides is the throughput — the one-way flow of matter and energy through the economy. Resources enter as low-entropy inputs (ordered, energy-rich), are transformed into goods and services, and leave as high-entropy waste (disordered, energy-poor). This throughput is not circular. It cannot be made circular. The 2nd Law of Thermodynamics says entropy always increases: you can recycle matter but you cannot recycle energy.
The sun is the only real income
Daly distinguishes between natural capital (the stock — forests, fisheries, aquifers, stable climate) and solar income (the flow — the renewable energy that arrives each day from the sun and powers photosynthesis, wind, rain cycles, and ultimately all biological productivity). A sustainable economy lives off solar income without depleting natural capital stocks. The current economy is drawing down stocks as if they were income — spending the principal and calling it revenue.
What "unpriced" actually means
When economists say a wetland or a forest is "unpriced," they mean it has no line in the circular flow model — no transaction, no price, no entry on a balance sheet. But the wetland is not unpriced because it has no value. It is unpriced because the model has no mechanism to see it. The standard model cannot register an asset that was never bought, never sold, and generates no cash flow — even if the entire regional economy depends on it for flood control, water filtration, and climate regulation.
