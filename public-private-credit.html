Public vs. Private Credit Creation

Who creates money, who charges interest, and where the interest goes — these are design choices, not natural laws.

Private banks create money by making loans. Every dollar created this way carries interest — and that interest flows to private shareholders. The structural growth imperative (models 3 and 4) follows directly from this design. But it isn't the only possible design. Public credit creation — through sovereign issuance, central bank lending, or public banks — can create money differently: with interest returning to the public, or with no interest obligation at all. The growth math changes when the money-creation mechanism changes.

The design space

Private bank credit (current system)
When a private bank makes a loan, it creates a deposit — money that didn't exist before. The borrower owes back principal plus interest. The interest flows to the bank's shareholders (after expenses and reserves). Because interest is charged on money that was newly created, total debt always exceeds total money in circulation. New credit creation must continuously outpace old debt repayment. This is the structural growth imperative. It is not a consequence of capitalism broadly — it's a consequence of this specific money-creation mechanism.
Public credit / sovereign issuance
Governments with sovereign currency can issue money directly — through spending, through the central bank, or through publicly owned banks. Interest on public loans returns to the public treasury rather than to shareholders. Alternatively, some public credit carries no interest at all (direct sovereign spending). In both cases, the structural interest gap is reduced or eliminated: the money created doesn't automatically demand more money than was created. This changes the growth math at the foundation.
Historical examples
The Bank of North Dakota (est. 1919) — the only US state-owned bank — returns profits to the state general fund rather than private shareholders. Colonial-era land banks and bills of credit issued by governments (before the federal constitution prohibited state-issued currency) functioned as public credit instruments. The post-WWII reconstruction programs in Japan and Germany used directed public credit to channel investment. Modern monetary theory (MMT) argues that sovereign currency issuers are already doing a version of public credit creation — but without accounting for it clearly.
What this model doesn't resolve
Public credit creation doesn't automatically solve the ecological limits problem — government spending can drive extraction just as readily as private lending. And public credit without discipline can produce inflation (too much money chasing too few goods). The question this model raises is narrower: whether the structural growth imperative built into private interest-bearing money creation is a necessary feature of any monetary system, or a specific design choice. The answer is: it's a design choice. What follows from that is a different conversation.
