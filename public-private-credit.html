Public vs. Private Credit Creation

Who creates money, who charges interest, and where the interest goes — these are design choices, not natural laws.

Private banks create money by making loans. Every dollar created this way carries interest — and that interest flows to private shareholders. But this isn't the only possible design. Public credit creation — through sovereign issuance, central bank lending, or public banks — creates money the same way, but with interest returning to the public. The growth math changes when that one variable changes.

The design space

Private bank credit (current system)
When a private bank makes a loan, it creates a deposit — money that didn't exist before. The borrower owes back principal plus interest. The interest flows to the bank's shareholders. Because interest is charged on newly created money, total debt always exceeds total money. New credit must continuously outpace old debt repayment. This is the structural growth imperative built into this specific design.
Public credit / sovereign issuance
Governments with sovereign currency can issue money directly — or through publicly owned banks. Interest on public loans returns to the public treasury as revenue. In some cases (direct sovereign spending), no interest is charged at all. In both cases, the structural interest gap is reduced or eliminated: the money created doesn't automatically demand more money than exists.
Historical examples
The Bank of North Dakota (est. 1919) — the only US state-owned bank — returns profits to the state general fund. Post-WWII reconstruction in Japan and Germany used directed public credit. Modern monetary theory (MMT) argues that sovereign currency issuers are already doing a version of public credit creation, just without accounting for it clearly.
What this model doesn't resolve
Public credit doesn't automatically solve ecological limits — government spending can drive extraction just as readily as private lending. And public credit without discipline can produce inflation. The question here is narrower: is the structural growth imperative a necessary feature of any monetary system, or a specific design choice? Answer: it's a design choice.
