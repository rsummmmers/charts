Reserve Requirements vs. Capital Requirements

Two different guardrails protecting against two different failures — and what neither one addresses.

The money multiplier model shows a reserve requirement — the fraction of deposits a bank must hold as cash. In the United States this is currently 0%. But banks are not unregulated: a different mechanism, the capital requirement, is now the primary regulatory tool. These are often conflated, but they address completely different problems.

Reserve Requirement — Liquidity
What it is
Cash or deposits held at the Federal Reserve
Measured against
Customer deposits (checking & savings accounts)
Protects against
Bank runs — customers demanding cash at once
US rate (2024)
0% — eliminated March 2020
Scenario it handles
100 depositors want their money back at the same time. If the bank has no reserves, it cannot pay them without selling assets or borrowing overnight. Reserve requirements (when they existed) ensured a cash buffer was always on hand.
What it doesn't cover
Whether the bank's loans are sound. A bank can have adequate reserves and still be technically insolvent — it has enough cash for today but its loan portfolio is worth less than what it owes.
Capital Requirement — Solvency
What it is
Equity — the bank's net worth (assets minus liabilities)
Measured against
Risk-weighted loans and investments
Protects against
Loan defaults — borrowers who never repay
Basel III CET1 minimum (2024)
4.5% of risk-weighted assets
Scenario it handles
A bank lends $100M in mortgages. 5% default — $5M in losses. Capital requirements ensure the bank has enough equity buffer to absorb that $5M without becoming insolvent. The losses come out of equity, not depositor funds.
What it doesn't cover
Whether the risk-weights assigned to assets are correct. In 2008, mortgage-backed securities were weighted as low-risk. Capital requirements said the banks were fine. They were not.
What neither guardrail addresses
The interest gap (models 3 & 4) Both mechanisms regulate bank safety — how banks survive bad outcomes. Neither addresses the structural fact that interest is charged on money that was never created. Reserve requirements and capital requirements are about who loses when loans go bad. They say nothing about the system-level growth imperative that interest creates.
What gets a loan (model 5) Capital requirements are calculated against "risk-weighted assets" — things with a market price and a measurable default probability. A wetland has no risk weight. A decade of unpaid care work has no risk weight. Ecological infrastructure does not appear in any capital ratio. The guardrails regulate what happens inside the model; they do not expand what the model can see.

Understanding the distinction

Liquidity vs. solvency — the core difference
Liquidity is about cash flow: can the bank pay its obligations today? Solvency is about net worth: is the bank worth more than it owes? A business can be solvent but illiquid (valuable assets, no cash) or liquid but insolvent (plenty of cash, but debts exceed assets). Banks can fail from either. The 2008 crisis involved both: illiquidity triggered by solvency concerns no one had properly measured.
Why the US moved to 0% reserve requirements
The Federal Reserve now controls monetary conditions through interest rates, not reserve ratios. By paying banks interest on reserves held at the Fed (currently ~3.65% IORB), it can incentivize banks to hold more or less cash without a legal mandate. This is a more flexible tool, but it means the money multiplier as classically taught (deposit × 1/reserve rate) is no longer mechanically enforced — it is now a policy choice expressed through price rather than quantity.
The problem with risk weights
Capital requirements depend on "risk-weighted assets" — each type of loan or investment is assigned a risk weight, and the bank must hold capital proportional to that weight. Government bonds are weighted near zero (assumed safe). Mortgages are weighted lower than business loans. The weights are set by regulators and rating agencies. In 2008, the weights were wrong. The mechanism is only as good as its model of risk — and its model of risk uses historical data from a world that no longer exists.
What this means for the other models
Model 1 (Money Multiplier) shows reserve requirements controlling the deposit cascade. That mechanism still operates conceptually — the cascade exists — but the enforced floor is now 0%. The real floor is set by capital requirements and by banks' own liquidity management. Models 3 and 4 (interest ratchet and interest variable) are unchanged by either guardrail: both mechanisms assume loans are repaid, and the structural interest gap exists regardless of how well-capitalized the bank is.
