Reserve vs. Capital Requirements

Two guardrails. Two different failure modes. One bank balance sheet.

Both reserve requirements and capital requirements involve banks holding something "back." But what they hold, what they're measured against, and what failure they prevent are completely different. The distinction becomes visible on a bank's balance sheet.

What neither guardrail addresses

The structural interest gap Both mechanisms regulate how banks survive bad outcomes. Neither changes the fact that interest is charged on money that was never created. A well-capitalized bank with adequate reserves still operates within a system where total debt must always exceed total money in existence.
What gets a loan Capital requirements are calculated against risk-weighted assets — things with a price and a measurable default rate. A wetland, a forest, a decade of unpaid care work: none have a risk weight. Both guardrails are calibrated to protect the financial system; neither expands what the financial system can see.

Understanding the distinction

Liquidity vs. solvency
Liquidity means having cash available today. Solvency means your assets exceed your liabilities. A bank can be solvent but illiquid (sound loans, but no cash for withdrawals) or liquid but insolvent (plenty of cash, but loans are worth less than deposits). Reserve requirements address liquidity. Capital requirements address solvency. The 2008 crisis involved both — illiquidity triggered by the discovery that solvency was far worse than reported.
Why the US moved to 0% reserve requirements
Since 2020, the Federal Reserve controls monetary conditions through the interest rate it pays banks on reserves held at the Fed (IORB, currently ~3.65%). This incentivizes banks to hold cash without a legal mandate. The classical money multiplier (1 ÷ reserve rate) still describes the conceptual mechanism, but the enforced floor is now zero — the actual floor is set by capital requirements and banks' own liquidity management.
The problem with risk weights
Capital requirements depend on "risk-weighted assets" — each loan type is assigned a weight, and the bank must hold capital proportional to it. Government bonds: near zero risk. Mortgages: moderate. The weights are set by regulators using historical default rates. In 2008, mortgage-backed securities were assigned low risk weights. They defaulted at catastrophic rates. The mechanism is only as good as its model of risk — and its model uses a past that may not resemble the future.
What this means for the other models
Model 1 shows reserve requirements at 10% — the historical US standard, useful for teaching the multiplier mechanics, but no longer the enforced rate. Model 7 (Risk Models) extends this: capital requirements calibrated to historical risk weights may be deeply wrong in a world where climate shifts the baseline distribution of defaults, floods, and crop failures. The guardrails were designed for a stable world.
