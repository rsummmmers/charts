Three Banking Guardrails

Reserve requirements, capital requirements, and loan loss reserves — three mechanisms protecting against three different failure modes.

Banks in the US are regulated by one of three agencies: the OCC (Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, part of Treasury) for nationally chartered banks, the Federal Reserve for state-chartered member banks, and the FDIC for state-chartered non-member banks. Not all banks are nationally chartered. All of them, regardless of charter, operate under three overlapping guardrail mechanisms — each designed to prevent a different kind of failure.

What these guardrails don't address

The structural interest gap All three mechanisms regulate how banks survive bad outcomes — runs, defaults, and expected credit losses. None of them change the fact that interest is charged on money created as debt. A well-capitalized, fully-provisioned bank with adequate reserves still operates inside a system where total debt must always exceed total money in existence.
What gets a loan Capital requirements and loan loss reserves are both calibrated against "risk-weighted assets" — things with a price and a measurable default rate. A wetland, a decade of unpaid care work, an intact watershed: none have a risk weight. All three guardrails protect the financial system from its own failures; none expand what the financial system can see.

Understanding the three mechanisms

Reserve requirements — liquidity guardrail
Reserve requirements mandate that banks hold a minimum fraction of deposits as cash. Their purpose: make sure the bank can pay customers who want their money back today. In the US, this requirement is currently 0% — eliminated in March 2020. The Federal Reserve now manages liquidity indirectly, by paying interest on reserves (IORB) to incentivize banks to hold cash voluntarily. The conceptual money multiplier (1 ÷ reserve rate) still describes the mechanism; the legal floor is now zero.
Capital requirements — solvency guardrail
Capital requirements mandate that banks hold a cushion of equity against their risk-weighted loan portfolio. Their purpose: if loans default, the equity absorbs losses before depositors are exposed. Under Basel III (the current international standard), banks must hold at least 4.5% Common Equity Tier 1 capital against risk-weighted assets — effectively ~7% including the conservation buffer. The weights are set by regulators using historical default rates. In 2008, mortgage-backed securities were assigned low risk weights. They defaulted at catastrophic rates. The guardrail is only as good as its model of risk.
Loan loss reserves (ACL) — credit quality guardrail
The Allowance for Credit Losses (ACL) is a bank-specific provision for expected losses in the loan portfolio — money set aside before losses happen, based on the bank's actual risk assessment. Unlike capital requirements (a fixed percentage set by Basel III), the ACL is set by bank examiners on a per-institution basis: a bank with riskier loans must hold more. It's dynamic — adjusted as the portfolio changes, as economic conditions shift, and as regulators reassess the bank's specific exposure. This is the third guardrail, and the least visible one from the outside.
Who regulates what
The OCC (Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, part of Treasury) regulates nationally chartered banks. The Federal Reserve regulates state-chartered member banks. The FDIC regulates state-chartered non-member banks. Not all banks are nationally chartered — there are thousands of state-chartered institutions that never touch OCC oversight. The OCC charters the banks that create money through lending; the OCC itself does not create money. All three agencies use the same framework (Basel III for capital, examiner discretion for ACL) but apply it to different slices of the banking system.
Liquidity vs. solvency vs. expected loss
Three different failure modes, three different mechanisms. Liquidity failure: the bank has the assets but can't pay today (solved by reserves). Solvency failure: liabilities exceed assets because loans went bad (solved by capital). Expected credit loss: known risk built into the existing portfolio (managed by ACL). The 2008 crisis involved all three simultaneously — illiquidity triggered by the discovery that solvency was far worse than reported, and that ACL provisions were dramatically understated because the risk models used historical data that didn't match what was actually in the portfolios.
What this means for the other models
Model 1 shows reserve requirements at 10% — the historical US standard, useful for teaching the multiplier mechanics, but no longer the enforced rate. Model 7 (Risk Models) extends the ACL problem: loan loss reserves calibrated to historical default rates may be deeply wrong in a world where climate shifts the baseline distribution of defaults, floods, and crop failures. All three guardrails were designed for a stationary world. The structural interest gap — models 3 and 4 — operates independently of all three. A perfectly regulated bank still participates in a system that requires continuous growth to remain solvent.
