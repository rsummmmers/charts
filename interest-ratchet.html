The Interest Ratchet

Why the gap between money that exists and money that is owed can only grow.

When a bank makes a loan, it creates the principal — the amount borrowed — and adds it to the economy. It does not create the interest. The interest must be repaid from money that already exists in the economy, or from money created by someone else taking out a new loan.

This means that at any given moment, the total debt in the system exceeds the total money available to repay it. To close the gap, someone must borrow more. But borrowing more creates new interest obligations. The gap grows. There is no stable state in which all existing debt can be repaid from existing money. This is not a policy failure. It is a structural feature of money created as interest-bearing debt.

The mechanism — and what it means

Why the ratchet only turns one way
A ratchet is a mechanical device that allows rotation in one direction and prevents it in the other. The interest obligation is the pawl — every time the economy tries to contract, the interest owed prevents full contraction. The gap closes only by creating more debt, which creates more gap. The lever has a direction and no reverse.
What partially mitigates it in practice
Banks spend their interest income back into the economy — as salaries, dividends, operating costs, and reinvestment. This recirculates money that can be used to pay interest, and it partially closes the gap without requiring new loans. But it depends on banks spending rather than hoarding, and it doesn't eliminate the structural growth requirement — it only delays it. As long as any money is held rather than circulated, the gap reasserts.
What would actually close the gap
Zero interest rates
Eliminates the gap but removes the incentive for lending. Not a stable state.
Government issues debt-free money
The MMT argument. Money created by public spending, not lending. Eliminates the interest problem but requires political will and inflation management.
Continuous economic growth
The current operating assumption. New production creates new income to service old debt. Works until it doesn't — in a physically finite world, this is a structural tension.
Banks spend all interest back
Partially true, partially mitigating. Not complete, not guaranteed.
The Daly connection
Herman Daly's critique of standard economics is that it treats the economy as the whole system and nature as an input. The interest ratchet makes this structural: because debt must always grow to service existing debt, the economy must always expand — extracting more resources, producing more goods, generating more cash flow to service the interest on the loans that financed the last round of extraction. A steady-state economy is incompatible with interest-bearing money creation as the primary mechanism of economic expansion. You cannot have infinite debt service in a finite physical world.
