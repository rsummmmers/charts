The Interest Ratchet

What happens to a fixed money supply when debt compounds.

When a bank makes a loan, it creates the principal — not the interest. The interest must be repaid from money already in circulation, or from someone else taking on new debt. Understanding the ratchet

The structural impossibility
In a closed system — fixed money supply — repaying interest-bearing debt is mathematically impossible. Only the principal was created; the interest was not. To pay the interest, someone must either default (destroying the debt without repayment) or take on new debt to cover it. But new debt carries new interest. The gap compounds. This is not a temporary crisis: it is a permanent feature of any monetary system where money is created as interest-bearing debt.
How the real system "solves" it
Real economies are not closed systems. New money is created continuously through new lending. The gap can be serviced by borrowing more — which is why total debt in most economies grows every year. Banks recycle interest income (as salaries, dividends, and operating costs) back into the economy, which partially recirculates it. The concern is not that individual loans fail to close — it is that the system requires continuous debt growth to remain functional. Growth is not a choice; it is a structural requirement.
What closes the gap in practice
Options for closing the gap, with their limits:
  • New borrowing — defers and compounds the gap
  • Income from real economic activity — works if the economy grows faster than interest
  • Bank profit recirculation — partial; banks spend interest on wages, dividends
  • Default / debt forgiveness — closes the gap, destroys the claim
  • Inflation — reduces real value of debt, transfers gap to savers
None of these close the gap permanently. They redistribute it, defer it, or destroy wealth in the process.
What this means at ecological limits
The "real economy grows faster than interest" solution requires perpetual economic growth. Economic growth requires throughput — matter and energy extracted from the biosphere (model 5). In a physically finite world, throughput cannot grow forever. At ecological limits, the standard escape valve closes. The structural growth imperative built into interest-bearing money becomes a direct driver of resource extraction — not because anyone chooses it, but because the math requires it.
