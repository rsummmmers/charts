Stock vs. Flow: Two Ways Water Can Fail

The Colorado River (flow-dependent) and the Quabbin Reservoir (managed stock) — same resource, different failure modes.

In systems thinking, a flow is a rate — water moving through a river right now. A stock is an accumulated quantity — water stored in a reservoir. These two types of resource have fundamentally different dynamics, different vulnerabilities, and different collapse patterns. Step through to see how each one fails under pressure.

Step 1 of 5
Flow system: Colorado River
Seven states, 40 million people, 5.5 million acres of farmland
The Colorado River is a flow system: its usability depends entirely on how much water is moving through it right now. If the flow drops, downstream users immediately lose access — there is no buffer.
Stock system: Quabbin Reservoir
412 billion gallons · supplies greater Boston since 1939
The Quabbin Reservoir is a stock system: it accumulates water over years and can supply cities even during dry stretches. But stocks have a ceiling — and once drawn down, recharge can take decades.
Stocks and flows fail differently. A flow system can fail instantly — cut the supply, lose the resource. A stock system fails slowly, then suddenly: the drawdown seems manageable until it isn't. Both failures are invisible until they arrive.
Flow
A rate of change over time. Measured in volume-per-time (acre-feet/year, gallons/day). Stops when the source stops. Examples: river discharge, rainfall, solar radiation, interest income, wage income.
Stock
An accumulated quantity. Measured in volume (acre-feet, gallons). Persists when inflow stops — but depletes if outflow exceeds inflow. Examples: reservoir storage, groundwater, topsoil, a savings account, biodiversity.
Flow failure
Immediate: when the source is stressed (drought, diversion), downstream users feel it at once. The Colorado's compact allocates more water than flows — any shortfall is instantly visible.
Stock failure
Gradual, then sudden: a reservoir can absorb years of deficit before the level becomes critical. When it crosses a threshold, the collapse can be rapid. The gap between "drawing down" and "crisis" masks the problem.
