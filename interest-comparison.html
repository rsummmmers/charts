The Interest Variable

Copying money is not the problem. This is.

The previous two models show that fractional reserve banking creates copies of money — and that those copies carry claims back to their origin. A common response is: "Yes, but loans get repaid. The system balances." That response is correct — but only for the principal. It misses the variable that makes the board impossible to clear.

This model holds everything constant and changes one thing: whether loans carry interest. In the interest-free world, every copy can be retired. The board clears. In the interest-bearing world, the copies can be retired but the interest cannot — because interest was never created. What this comparison isolates

The copying is not the flaw
Interest-free credit creation — money lent into existence and retired when repaid — is mathematically coherent. The copies are real, they circulate, and they can all return. If the only objection to fractional reserve banking were the multiplication of money, that objection would be answerable. Copying, by itself, is a design choice that can work.
Interest on created money is the specific flaw
When interest is charged on money that was itself created as debt, the system requires more money to exist than was ever created. That shortfall cannot be covered by any individual actor. It can only be covered by new borrowing — which creates new interest. The gap between money in existence and money owed is not a temporary imbalance. It is the permanent operating state of the system.
Why this is harder to argue than it sounds
The mainstream response is that banks recirculate interest income — through salaries, dividends, and operating costs — back into the economy, providing the money needed to service debt. This is true and partially mitigating. The counterpoint is that it depends on continuous circulation and continuous growth. A steady-state economy cannot service interest-bearing debt at scale. The growth imperative is not a policy preference — it is baked into the math.
What changes if you accept this argument
If the structural growth requirement is real, then the ecological critique of infinite growth isn't just about resource limits — it's about monetary architecture. You cannot have a steady-state economy and an interest-bearing debt-money system simultaneously. One of them has to give. That is not a claim about individual behavior or corporate greed. It is a claim about system design.
