Bucket-nomics

Buy the vessel once. The input is free.

Some infrastructure works by capturing a free, ongoing input — rain, sunlight, wind, ecosystem services. You buy the vessel once. After that, the input just arrives. The economy has trouble pricing this because it is used to valuing things by what they cost to operate, not what they cost to own. When the operating cost is zero, the standard frame produces the wrong answer.

Part 1 — The bucket

The clearest version: a bucket and rain. One purchase. Then the rain just falls. The question the standard economic frame asks — "does the value of what the bucket catches justify its price at current water rates?" — is the wrong question. The right question is: what does it cost to never buy a bucket?

Part 2 — The pattern

The bucket isn't special. The same structure appears across solar panels, wind turbines, cisterns, aquifers, and living systems. In each case: one upfront cost, then a free and ongoing input — sunlight, wind, rain, groundwater, ecological services. The alternative in each case is to keep purchasing the input, at a price that rises over time.

The pricing problem

What the commodity price actually measures
The price of municipal water measures the cost of extraction, treatment, and delivery of water collected elsewhere and piped to you. It does not measure: the value of water for plant growth, the ecological cost of the source watershed, what water will cost in 10 years as aquifers decline, the value of water on-site vs. water delivered, or the resilience value of not depending on a centralized system. Historical commodity prices are receipts for past transactions. They don't price future flows.
What the living system actually produces
A rain garden, a rain barrel, a greywater loop, a food forest — these are not water-purchasing alternatives. They are systems. They produce water, but also: improved water quality (no chlorine, naturally filtered), cooling through evapotranspiration, soil building, pollinator habitat, flood mitigation (reducing peak runoff), aesthetic and psychological value, and resilience against supply disruption. None of these appear in a simple payback calculation against current municipal rates.
The rising price problem
Municipal water prices in the US have risen 50–70% over the past decade in many cities, and are expected to continue rising as infrastructure ages and source water becomes scarcer. A payback calculation using today's price understates the value of a system that locks in zero marginal cost. The longer the horizon, the more the commodity line accelerates and the system line stays flat.
Bucket-nomics as a general frame
The bucket-nomics frame applies anywhere a living system is being evaluated against commodity substitution: a food forest vs. a grocery bill, a wetland vs. a stormwater system, a healthy fishery vs. aquaculture, topsoil vs. synthetic fertilizer. In each case, the standard economic frame uses historical commodity prices as the baseline, ignores the full output of the living system, ignores price trajectories, and ignores what the system does to the conditions around it. The bucket is the system. The rain is everything it produces that the price doesn't see.
