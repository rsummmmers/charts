Risk Models and a Changing World

Financial risk models are built on historical data. What happens when history stops being a reliable guide to the future?

Every major financial institution uses quantitative risk models to assess how much capital to hold, which loans to approve, and how exposed they are to loss. These models share a common assumption: that the distribution of future outcomes will resemble the distribution of past outcomes. In a stable world, this is reasonable. In a world where the underlying physical conditions are shifting, it fails — silently, and at scale.

Step 1 of 5
The silent failure mode: A risk model that is running on a shifted distribution does not produce an error message. It produces a number — a VaR figure, a stress test result, a capital ratio — that looks exactly like a correct answer. The model is confident. The confidence is unfounded. The gap between the model's world and the actual world widens as conditions change, and the model has no mechanism to detect this. It will only become visible after the fact, in the losses.

The stationarity problem in practice

What VaR actually measures
Value at Risk (VaR) is the standard financial risk metric. A 99% VaR of $10M means: based on historical data, there is a 1% chance of losing more than $10M on any given day. The critical words are "based on historical data." VaR doesn't predict the future — it describes the past, then assumes the future will look like it. When it doesn't, VaR is precisely wrong, not roughly wrong.
Stress tests and their model
Annual bank stress tests (Dodd-Frank, Basel III) run banks through "severe but plausible" scenarios. The scenarios are built from historical data: the worst recession in the last 30 years, the worst housing crash, the worst credit crunch. But "worst in the last 30 years" is not the same as "worst that can happen." It is the worst that has happened in a period of relative ecological and climate stability that is now ending.
What changes when the distribution shifts
When "100-year floods" occur every 10 years, flood insurance is systematically underpriced. When crop yield variance increases, agricultural lending risk is systematically underestimated. When wildfire seasons extend, property values in affected areas are systematically overvalued as collateral. Each of these is a case where the historical distribution gives a number that looks rigorous but is wrong — and where the error compounds across the entire system simultaneously.
Why this matters for the other models
Capital requirements (model 6) use risk-weighted assets — and the weights are derived from historical default rates. If default rates in certain asset classes are about to rise due to climate-driven physical damage, the capital held against those assets is inadequate. The guardrail was calibrated to the old world. This is not a failure of individual banks being reckless: it is a structural failure of the entire risk modeling framework applied to a non-stationary world.
