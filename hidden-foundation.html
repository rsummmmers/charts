The Hidden Foundation

The price of things is low because most of the cost isn't in the price.

The standard economy prices what it can capture: labor that's waged, energy that's extracted, goods that are sold. But a large fraction of what makes production possible — care work that reproduces the workforce, ecological services that supply clean inputs, and fossil energy representing hundreds of millions of years of stored solar work — is priced at zero or near zero. The cheapness of things is not efficiency. It is a transfer.

The three hidden inputs

Fossil energy: 300 million years, extraction price
Coal, oil, and gas are ancient sunlight — solar energy captured by living systems over hundreds of millions of years and stored in chemical form. The market prices fossil fuels at extraction cost: the labor and capital to get them out of the ground. It does not price the time it took to form them, the impossibility of replacement, or the full damage from burning them. The result: energy appears cheaper than it is by any meaningful measure of what it actually costs.
Unpaid care work: ~$10–39 trillion/year globally
Every worker who shows up to a waged job was fed, clothed, raised, educated, and emotionally sustained by someone — usually women, usually unpaid. Care work, domestic labor, and community maintenance reproduce the workforce. Without it, no waged economy functions. The ILO estimates unpaid care work at 10–39% of global GDP, depending on methodology. None of it appears in GDP. It doesn't show up in prices. It is the substrate on which the priced economy sits.
Ecological services: ~$125–145 trillion/year (Costanza et al.)
The economy takes clean water, clean air, stable climate, pollination, flood regulation, and soil fertility as given. These are not free — they are produced continuously by living systems. Costanza et al. estimated global ecosystem services at $125–145 trillion per year in 2014, larger than global GDP at the time. When these services degrade, costs appear — as disaster, as medical bills, as infrastructure replacement — but the value intact never appears in any price.
The permanomics question
These three inputs share a structure: they are real, they are finite or degradable, they are consumed by the economy, and they are not in the price. The economy looks productive because it doesn't pay for the most important things it uses. Permanomics asks what the system would look like if it did — not by taxing damage after the fact, but by starting from the full value of what's being used and not used up.
